Tuesday, 11 August 2026 &mdash; Horizon Quantum Computing (HQ) used Canaccord Genuity’s 46th Annual Growth Conference to outline a long-term plan to become a foundational software layer for quantum computing, even as the company remains pre-revenue and says the industry has not yet reached genuine quantum advantage. Shares rose 2.15% in premarket trading to $12.83 from $12.56, though the stock remains well below its 52-week high of $45. The stock has declined 17.6% over the past week and trades 72% below its 52-week peak, reflecting what InvestingPro data identifies as high price volatility&mdash;a pattern that has characterized the stock’s recent performance. With a market capitalization of $677.89 million and a beta of just 0.3, the company shows relatively low correlation to broader market movements despite sharp individual price swings.

CEO and founder Dr. Joe Fitzsimons said the company is trying to solve a basic problem in the field: quantum hardware has advanced, but the software needed to turn that hardware into useful systems is still immature. He described Horizon as a software business with different economics from quantum hardware makers, while warning that the market is still early and that near-term revenue can be misleading.

Dr. Fitzsimons said he entered quantum computing in 2004, earned a PhD at Oxford, held a research fellowship at Merton College and later built a research group in Singapore beginning in 2010. He said he saw a gap in the market: hardware companies were racing to build quantum processors, but the software layer needed to use those machines was underdeveloped.

He said the field began to change around 2015 and 2016, when systems from IBM Quantum Experience and the University of Bristol showed better error rates, moving from roughly 30% fidelity to about 99% fidelity. That shift, he said, made frontier progress move from academia into industry. He left a tenured academic role in 2018 to start Horizon and said he believes he was the first tenured professor to do so in quantum computing.

Horizon&rsquo;s central argument is that today&rsquo;s quantum programs are still too limited. Dr. Fitzsimons said many are little more than circuits: a sequence of operations followed by measurement. In his view, useful quantum computing will require reinventing about 80 years of computer science and software engineering, including operating system kernels, dynamic memory allocation, input and output, and network communications.

Dr. Fitzsimons said on-premises systems matter because they allow deeper integration with control hardware and avoid software layers that can hide what the machine can really do. He said this also helps the company test capabilities safely, without risking damage to scarce machines.

Horizon also discussed its work with Quantum Machines on embedded calibration, a problem that can force quantum systems offline and reduce their availability.

Today, quantum computers often need to be taken out of service for calibration runs and measurements to track drift and tune parameters. Dr. Fitzsimons said that is a poor fit for production use, especially if a machine is expected to run business-critical jobs continuously.

The company&rsquo;s approach is to keep calibration running continuously using results from normal operation. That, it says, should reduce downtime, improve reliability and lower operating costs. Horizon said this capability is part of its broader code, compile and deploy platform, which it calls Triple Alpha.

Horizon said it is deliberately staying pre-revenue and pre-advantage. Dr. Fitzsimons said any revenue earned before quantum advantage would likely come from professional services and system access, which he described as misleading because it would not reflect the step-change value the company expects later.

He compared the situation to early and later stages of artificial intelligence, saying the difference between current quantum systems and future ones is like the gap between GPT-1 or GPT-2 and GPT-5. In his view, the market should not confuse early pilot work with a fully working technology platform.

The company is also taking a narrower customer approach than some rivals. Rather than focusing first on broad developer adoption, it is concentrating on hardware manufacturers and a small set of internal applications. Dr. Fitzsimons said broad enterprise adoption is unlikely to scale until quantum computers can solve real problems and produce value for end users.

Horizon said it does not agree with the common view that quantum computing will be limited to four areas: optimization, simulation, machine learning and cryptography. Dr. Fitzsimons said the company looks at program structure more broadly and tries to replace the loops and recursive functions that dominate runtime with quantum-efficient alternatives.

He said that could apply to a wider set of problems, including large inverse problems in oil and gas and other use cases that have not yet been fully mapped out. But he also said the industry is still at a stage where no one is using quantum computers in production to solve hard problems that affect the bottom line.

To help close that gap, Horizon recently added an internal applications team. The team is focused on three high-value problems across three industries. Dr. Fitzsimons said the company believes this is a better near-term path than trying to win short-term enterprise engagements that are unlikely to reach meaningful commercial scale.

Horizon did not report financial results on the call, but management emphasized its operating discipline. The company described itself as a software business with economics that differ from capital-intensive hardware companies. The balance sheet reflects this approach: InvestingPro data shows Horizon holds more cash than debt, with a debt-to-equity ratio of just 0.01 and a current ratio of 33.84, indicating liquid assets far exceed short-term obligations. The company’s Financial Health score rates as "WEAK" primarily due to its pre-revenue status and negative cash flow of $13.05 million over the last twelve months, though analysts do not anticipate profitability this year.

Dr. Fitzsimons said Horizon is keeping operating expenses tightly controlled while it remains pre-revenue and pre-advantage. He said spending should not grow without bound during this phase.

He also pointed to a long-term model that resembles Microsoft&rsquo;s path from programming languages and interpreters to system software and then desktop applications. In his view, if Horizon&rsquo;s strategy works, the company could eventually reach billions of users, though he said that remains a long-term goal.

For now, the company is betting that the next phase of quantum computing will be defined less by raw hardware alone and more by the software, calibration and control systems that sit around it.
