In December 2024, the Virginia Joint Legislative Audit and Review Commission released the results of a year-long
study of data center industry impacts in Virginia. The results concluded that data centers had been paying their full
cost of electric service but foreshadowed that additional reforms could be necessary to ensure fairness going
forward.

To ensure data centers and other high load customers continue to pay their fair share and to mitigate against
stranded costs, the Company proposed, and the SCC approved, a robust package of cost allocation, rate design,
and Terms and Conditions changes in the Company’s biennial review proceeding, Case No. PUR-2025-00058.
Notably, the Company’s proposals included the creation of a new customer class for its most energy-intensive
customers. The criteria for inclusion in the new class (called GS-5) are: 1) a contracted or measured electric
demand equal to or greater than 25 megawatts, and 2) an anticipated or measured electric load factor of 75% or
more. While data centers generally fit this profile of high demand paired with a high load factor, the new GS-5 class
is not exclusive to data centers. Any customer that meets both criteria will take service under GS-5 on and after
January 1, 2027, except for customers meeting certain employment provisions that exercise(d) a one-time opt-out
provision provided for under Virginia law.

3
When determining electric rates, it is important to examine both the costs that are eligible to be recovered from the
Company’s customers and how those costs should be allocated among customer classes. By concentrating the
most energy-intensive users in the GS-5 class, the Company will enable greater transparency and precision when
those cost-allocation decisions are made.

In addition to authorizing the creation of the GS-5 class, the SCC also approved a package of new and strengthened
contractual provisions for high-load customers including long-term contracts, minimum charges, collateral
requirements, and exit fees. With limited exceptions, they apply to all customers, individually or on contiguous
property, under any rate schedule with actual or contracted electric demand greater than or equal to 25 megawatts.
These provisions protect residential and other customers from bearing infrastructure costs driven by high-load
customers. They function by creating binding financial commitments, which ensure that high-load customers
continue paying their fair share of electric utility infrastructure costs.

Effective January 2027, high-load customers will face minimum charges that set a floor for what they pay, which is
based on their contracted demand amount. In other words, a high-load customer will be billed higher power
generation and delivery charges if their actual usage falls below certain minimum thresholds relative to the amount
of power they requested. By strengthening the financial commitments required of high-load customers, the customer
is incentivized to request only the amount of power they realistically will need. This helps mitigate concerns about
“overbuilding” the electric system and risk that other customers could face additional costs if revenue collected from
high-load customers is insufficient to cover the demands they place on the system.

A 14-year contract term backed by collateral requirements for new, high-load customers also protects others from
adverse rate impacts where the customer ceases taking service from the Company before this initial contract term is
complete. This standardized contract term interacts with the minimum demand charges, as well as exit fee

Consistent with other aspects of the SCC-approved package, the extended contract term (i.e., 14 years),  exit fees

Electric rates (typically expressed as cents per kilowatt-hour of usage) are used across the industry as the point of
comparison instead of total electric bills because total electric bills do not provide a consistent measure of
affordability across customers with different energy use patterns. For instance, many Virginians and North
Carolinians use electricity for both summer cooling and winter heating, while customers in other states with colder
climates such as in the Northeast and Mid-West rely to a greater extent on natural gas, propane, or fuel oil for winter
heating. Because those winter heating services are billed separately, their costs are not captured in a direct
comparison of total electric bills. Conversely, the comparison of electric rates presents a clear picture of the per-unit
cost of electric service, independent of customers’ reliance on electricity rather than other fuels, how much square
footage they are heating or cooling, the age of the housing stock relative to other jurisdictions, etc.
The stability of the Company’s electric rates can be expressed as a compound annual growth rate (“CAGR”).
Between January 2016 and January 2026, the rate paid by a typical residential customer of Dominion Energy
increased by about a 4.18% CAGR, while the rate paid by a typical large industrial customer increased on a
compound annual basis by about 4.17%. Over the same period, the Energy category within the Consumer Price
Index, a proxy for inflation, increased by a CAGR of 4.23%.

Capacity Planning
As a member of PJM, Dominion Energy is a signatory to PJM’s Reliability Assurance Agreement, which obligates
the Company to purchase sufficient capacity to maintain overall system reliability. PJM determines these obligations
for each zone using its annual load forecast and reserve margin5 guidelines as inputs. PJM then conducts a
capacity auction process for meeting these input requirements up to three years into the future. This auction process
includes the Base Residual Auction (“BRA”) for the RPM as well as subsequent incremental auctions that are held
to allow market sellers and PJM to adjust positions for changes such as load forecasts, generator retirements,
ELCC, construction delays, or outage assumptions. This auction process determines the clearing reserve margin
and the capacity price for each zone for the delivery year that is three years in the future.
The Company is required to provide sufficient generation to cover its load obligation, which is calculated using
PJM’s most current load forecast and planning parameters such as ELCC, reserve margin requirements, and
transmission import limitations.

Dominion Energy uses PJM’s reserve margin guidelines to determine its long-term capacity requirement. PJM
conducts an annual reserve requirement study to determine an adequate level of capacity in its footprint to meet the
target level of reliability, measured as a loss of load expectation equivalent to one day of outage in ten years.
PJM develops reserve margin estimates for planning (i.e., delivery) years (June through May) rather than calendar
years. Because PJM is a summer peaking entity, and because the summer period of PJM’s planning year coincides
with the calendar year summer period, calendar and planning year reserve requirement estimates are determined
based on the identical summer period. For example, the Company uses PJM’s 2026/2027 delivery year
assumptions for the 2026 calendar year in this 2026 IRP because it represents the expected peak load during the
summer of 2026.

On July 14, 2026, PJM published the results of the BRA for the 2028/2029 Delivery Year. The results showed that
the entire PJM footprint again cleared at the FERC-approved capacity price cap, with a clearing price of
$325.00/MW-day, a modest 2.5% decrease from the $333.44/MW-day clearing price established in the 2027/2028
BRA.6 Similar to the prior auction, the DOM Zone did not separate from the remainder of PJM, and no other PJM
load deliverability areas experienced price separation.

Despite the relatively small change in clearing price, the 2028/2029 BRA reinforced concerns regarding PJM’s long-
term supply-demand balance. PJM procured 138,317.8 MW of unforced capacity (“UCAP”) through the RPM
auction, while an additional 10,863.8 MW of UCAP was supplied through the FRR construct, resulting in total
committed resources of 149,181.6 MW UCAP. However, PJM determined that its reliability requirement for the
Delivery Year was approximately 156,013 MW UCAP, leaving the region over 6,800 MW below the resource level
needed to satisfy PJM’s one-in-ten-year reliability standard. This marked the second consecutive auction in which
PJM was effectively unable to procure sufficient resources to meet its target reliability requirement and represented
the second auction in which the entire RTO fell materially short of the planning reserve target.
The shortfall was driven largely by continued growth in forecasted electricity demand, particularly from large load
additions and data center development, which increased PJM’s reliability requirement by more than 3,600 MW
relative to the prior auction. PJM reported that supply offered into the capacity market increased by approximately
3,447 MW UCAP, while forecasted load increased by 1,375 MW.

The 2028/2029 BRA also highlighted the growing gap between PJM’s IRM requirement and the level of capacity
ultimately committed through the auction. PJM’s reliability studies indicated that an IRM of 20.0% was necessary to
maintain the one-day-in-ten-year Loss of Load Expectation reliability standard for the 2028/2029 Delivery Year.
However, the auction cleared at an IRM of 14.4%, or 5.6% below that target. The widening gap reflects the
combined effects of accelerating load growth, resource accreditation changes under ELCC methodologies, and the
cumulative impact of more than a decade of thermal unit retirements, with replacement resource additions and
accreditation not fully offsetting those losses. While recent PJM and stakeholder initiatives have increasingly
focused on reforming reliability planning assumptions, accreditation methodologies, and market design constructs,
current auction results highlight the fundamental challenge PJM faces in maintaining adequate reserve margins
across the region.

PJM estimated that absent the FERC-approved price cap, the auction would have cleared at approximately
$554.72/MW-day.

For Dominion Energy, the 2028/2029 BRA results reinforce the importance of maintaining and developing
accredited capacity resources within the Company’s service territory. Continued load growth coupled with
dispatchable generation retirements, increasing reserve requirements, and evolving ELCC accreditation
methodologies have driven PJM-wide capacity shortages and a growing divergence between procured capacity and
system reliability requirements. As a net importer of energy and a region with PJM’s highest projected load growth,
the DOM Zone currently remains dependent on sufficient generating resources both within and outside the
Company’s footprint. These results support the importance of continued evaluation of generation additions, storage,
demand-side resources, transmission enhancements, and market purchases as part of Dominion Energy’s long-
term resource planning process.

The entire PJM region is experiencing unprecedented load growth, which results in challenges in securing capacity
resources needed to meet that growing demand. This challenge is exacerbated by (i) significant loss of dispatchable
generation capacity throughout PJM due to premature retirements, and (ii) the composition of PJM's interconnection
queue that continues to be dominated by solar, wind, energy storage, and hybrid resources, while comparatively
fewer new dispatchable thermal resources are advancing through the queue.

A significant portion of PJM's forecasted load growth is associated with large-load additions, particularly data
centers and other energy-intensive customers. In response, PJM has undertaken several initiatives to refine load
forecasting methodologies, improve how large-load additions are assessed, forecasted, and incorporated into
planning studies, and evaluate mechanisms that better align new load additions with available generation and
transmission infrastructure. PJM and stakeholders continue to evaluate policies related to load forecasting accuracy,
customer commitment requirements, curtailment provisions, and alternative approaches that may allow certain large
loads to coordinate generation development with load additions. These efforts reflect growing recognition that the
magnitude and pace of forecasted demand growth introduce additional uncertainty into long-term resource
adequacy planning and may materially influence future capacity requirements, transmission expansion needs, and
market outcomes.

PJM's recent capacity auction results underscore the growing challenge of maintaining adequate accredited
capacity to satisfy reliability requirements. Rapid load growth, generator retirements, and evolving ELCC
accreditation methodologies have reduced available accredited capacity relative to projected demand growth.
Although substantial quantities of new generation are proposed across the PJM region, many projects remain under
development, and a significant portion of future additions consist of resource types that receive lower accredited
capacity values than traditional dispatchable generation. As a result, increases in installed generating capability do
not necessarily produce corresponding increases in accredited capacity, contributing to tighter reserve margins and
heightened resource adequacy concerns.

4.5.6  Natural Gas Resources
Natural gas resources are essential for grid reliability and the energy transition because they are dispatchable
resources that work in tandem with intermittent renewable generation. Their flexible operating characteristics allow
them to follow load and generate electricity when it is needed, including during periods of peak demand or when
renewable resources are unavailable. Natural gas units can quickly start, ramp output up or down, and shut off as
conditions change, helping to balance large swings in renewable energy production and mitigating the risk of
intermittent generation to maintain a reliable electric grid.

As baseload demand continues to grow, additional generation resources will be needed to ensure the electric
system can reliably meet increasing customer energy requirements. Rising demand places greater pressure on
existing generation assets and reduces the margin available to respond to peak conditions, equipment outages, or
fluctuations in renewable energy output. Expanding generation capacity, particularly with dispatchable resources
that can operate when needed, will help maintain grid reliability, support projected load growth, and ensure
adequate energy is available under a wide range of operating conditions.

than traditional turbine technologies. By producing more electricity from the same amount of natural gas, advanced-
class units reduce operating costs and improve market competitiveness, particularly for a baseload facility. Higher
efficiency also lowers carbon emissions per megawatt-hour, helping support environmental and regulatory
objectives while maximizing the value of available pipeline gas capacity. Advanced-class turbines are well suited for
large-scale combined-cycle configurations because their higher output allows a plant to achieve maximum output
with fewer generating units, reducing overall plant complexity and maintenance requirements.
The Company is currently considering a combined-cycle (“CC”) approach for new baseload natural gas generation
facilities. A CC power plant is typically the preferred choice when the system needs a large amount of reliable,
dispatchable energy that will run frequently throughout the year. Unlike a simple-cycle combustion turbine (“CT”), a
CC plant captures the exhaust heat from the gas turbines and uses it to produce steam that drives a steam turbine,
generating additional electricity from the same fuel. This significantly improves efficiency, reduces fuel costs, and
lowers emissions per megawatt-hour.

CTs are generally developed when the primary need is peaking capacity, rapid startup capability, or operational
flexibility rather than low-cost energy production. They are less expensive to construct, can be brought online more
quickly, and are well suited for serving peak demand periods, supporting renewable integration, and maintaining
reliability during extreme weather events. For that reason, facilities such as the Chesterfield Energy Reliability
Center are being developed with simple-cycle CT technology to provide flexible reliability support, while larger
combined-cycle projects are intended to meet the system’s growing energy requirements over the long term.
To meet the energy and capacity needs associated with the load forecast, and in the absence of a commercially
viable carbon-free, dispatchable generation alternative, natural gas generation will remain a critical component of
the Company's strategy to reliably meet generation demand.

Plan Sensitivities on Load Forecast
First, the Company conducted sensitivities using different load forecasts. The Company used the same general
methodology to create the high/low sensitivities as it did in the PJM Derived Load Forecast. The differences are that
the high/low sensitivities use a variation on the Data Center Load Forecast and the EE adjustment to the load
forecast. In the high load forecast sensitivity, the Company scaled the PJM Data Center Load Forecast such that it
was consistent with the Company’s internal 95
th percentile data center forecast. Additionally, the Company modeled
that EE savings would be half of the forecasted amounts. This resulted in a high load forecast sensitivity that starts
out 3% higher than the PJM Derived Load Forecast in the first year, moving to 14% higher by 2040.
The low load forecast sensitivity used the same general methodology as in the high load forecast sensitivity, with the
exception that the PJM Data Center Load Forecast was scaled to be consistent with the Company’s internal 5
th
percentile data center forecast. Similarly, EE savings were increased by 50%. This resulted in a low load forecast
sensitivity that starts off 2% lower than the PJM Derived Load Forecast in the first year, moving to 13% lower by
2040.

HB 895/SB 448 significantly expanded the energy storage deployment targets for the Company, including adding
new requirements for long-duration energy storage targets. Under the amended statute, the Company must petition

pursuant to Enactment Clause 4 of HB 895/SB 448, the long-duration energy storage target does not become

reasonably achievable. The SCC must issue its determination in that proceeding by March 1, 2031, and may
propose modified targets and interim targets for long-duration energy storage. Additionally, during that same
proceeding, the SCC must review the short-duration energy storage targets and may adjust the targets based on the
status of available energy storage technologies and the benefits of energy storage to customers and to the security
and reliability of the electric grid.

PJM Derived Load Forecast for DOM LSE

The Company utilized the PJM DOM Zone Load Forecast as published in the 2026 PJM Load Forecast
Report, dated January 14, 2026, in the development of the 2026 IRP Portfolios. PJM annually solicits
information from its member electric distribution companies (“EDC”) and/or Load Serving Entities (“LSE”)
regarding significant future block load increases, including data centers, that are known to the EDCs. The
Company provided PJM the data center load forecast for its customers located in the DOM LSE including
the retail choice customers (“Choice customers”). PJM then independently analyzed this and other LSE
submissions, incorporated the information into its annual forecast at its discretion, developed a load forecast
for all load zones within the PJM footprint, and published its report on the PJM website (the “PJM Dom Zone
Load Forecast”). The DOM Zone is one of these load zones.

As in prior IRPs, in order to properly use the PJM DOM Zone Load Forecast for modeling purposes,
Dominion Energy converted that forecast to the DOM LSE level. This conversion is necessary for modeling
purposes because it provides the Company’s customers’ demand (i.e., those customers the Company is
obligated to serve). The PJM DOM Zone Load Forecast, in contrast, provides the demand for the entire
DOM Zone, which includes customers served by other LSEs. The Company refers to this load forecast as
the 2026 PJM Derived Load Forecast.

To prepare the PJM Derived Load Forecast for the DOM LSE, the Company used the following process as
diagrammed in Figure 4A-2 below.

 Figure 4A-2: PJM DOM Zone Load Forecast conversion to DOM LSE Forecast flow chart

The Company first scaled down the PJM DOM Zone peak load and energy forecasts to the DOM LSE level.
The Company completed this in two parts. First, the Company adjusted the forecast by taking out PJM’s
DOM Zone data center forecast and then applied a normalized historical DOM LSE-to-DOM Zone load ratio.
The Company calculated the ratio by using a two-year average (2024 and 2025) of DOM LSE-to-DOM Zone
annual energy, excluding data centers. This method of scaling down the PJM forecast ensures that the DOM
LSE-to-DOM Zone ratio appropriately changes in the forecast period.

Second, the Company then added back the DOM LSE portion of the data center forecast and removed the
Choice forecast. Note that the data center forecast the Company incorporated into the PJM Derived Load

Forecast is produced by PJM, includes only the DOM LSE data center forecast, and does not include the
forecast from NOVEC or other cooperatives in the DOM Zone. Finally, the Company removed all additional
DSM savings incremental to 2025 (“incremental DSM”) to result in the PJM Derived Load Forecast used for
the 2026 IRP.

Overall, the 2026 PJM Derived Load Forecast anticipates a 2.8% and 3.8% CAGR for the DOM LSE
summer non-coincident peak (“NCP”) demand and annual energy, respectively, over the Planning Period
(2026-2046). Over the same period, the 2026 Company Load Forecast predicts a 2.5% and 3.4% CAGR for
the DOM LSE summer non-coincident peak demand and annual energy, respectively. Forecasts for both
energy (megawatt-hour (“MWh”)) and peaks (“MW”) are presented. As shown in Figure 4A-3 below, the

As described in Figure 4A-2, the transition from the DOM Zone forecast to the DOM LSE forecast includes
a reduction for net incremental DSM. However, the effects of DSM and broader energy efficiency initiatives
may already be reflected in the DOM Zone forecast through the historical load data used in PJM’s forecast
development. Over the past several years, increasing adoption of energy-efficient technologies and
programs has contributed to lower load growth, and these impacts are inherently represented within
historical trends. Accordingly, the incremental DSM adjustment may partially overlap with efficiency-related
effects already embedded in the PJM forecast, contributing to a potential downward bias in the DOM LSE
forecast. The Company will continue to evaluate opportunities to refine its forecasting methodology and
assumptions to appropriately account for these effects and mitigate the potential for forecast bias.

The 2026 IRP also includes the Company’s internally developed peak demand and energy forecast for the
DOM LSE (i.e., the Company Load Forecast). The Company Load Forecast includes forecasts of sales and

conducted a study to forecast EVs that is specific to its service territory, PJM has not been able to conduct
such a detailed study for each LSE in its load zones. Additionally, the Company is able to adjust its internal
load forecast to account for the impact of the incremental adoption of rooftop solar. Furthermore, converting

Data Center Forecasting for the DOM LSE
The Company has seen unprecedent levels of growth in both the size and number of requests for delivery
points in the last several years, including the significant increase in volume and increased size from large-
load customers. As of February 2026, approximately 25,000 megawatts of large-load delivery point requests
have been received with projected connection dates through December 31, 2031. Those requests continue
to grow. Importantly, however, the 2025 forecast was not based on contracts. Dominion Energy uses a
systematic process, refined over several years, to develop a data center billing demand forecast
First, Dominion Energy identified the seven largest and/or fastest growing data center customers, creating
seven customer specific segments. Next, it combined all other data center customers (approximately 47)
into an eighth segment. The Company calculated an initial billing demand forecast for the eight customer
segments by preparing three different demand forecasts (linear regression, polynomial regression, bass
diffusion) for each customer segment resulting in 24 different demand models. Next, the Company applied
customer intelligence (publicly available information, internal project communications with the customer, and
industry reports, and customer provided forecasts) to select the appropriate demand model for each of the
customer segments. If none of the three models aligned with customer intelligence as to future business
growth, then an adjusted growth curve was used (e.g., a flat growth curve). The Company then used the
historical monthly usage of demand to create the forecasted demand values by month within each year for
each customer segment.

The DOM LSE Data Center Contracting Process
The Contracting Process for Data Centers3
The connection process begins when a data center submits a request for service to Dominion Energy’s
distribution team. The request must include a site plan or building layout, requested energization date, the
capacity being requested including a ramp schedule, and meter voltage (primary or secondary voltage).
The Company reviews this request and provides information to the customer, including a feasibility report to
identify any critical flaws and high-level needs for energization. Upon receipt of this information, the
customer decides whether to proceed to an Engineering Letter of Authorization (“ELOA”).
Figure 4A-10 provides a brief overview of each step of a typical data center request process. These steps
are also described in the subsequent narrative.

After the project moves through the queue process and all engineering is completed, the customer is offered
a Construction Letter of Agreement (“CLOA”). Here again, the customer must decide whether to proceed by
signing the CLOA within 90 days of issuance. The CLOA contracts Dominion Energy to build infrastructure—
therefore, no significant capital investments are made until the customer executes this agreement.
Importantly, the CLOA triggers three items, deposits for long lead time equipment, 100% reimbursement of
all costs incurred if the project is canceled before energization, and the requirement to execute an Electric
Service Agreement upon energization for the installed capacity. If a customer requests additional
infrastructure beyond what is normally provided for new service connections, then that customer pays the
incremental cost of that infrastructure via a facilities charge, as provided for in the Company’s approved
Terms and Conditions.

The Company (or customer) will pursue substation permitting with the locality. In parallel, if required, the
Company will file an application for a certificate of public convenience and necessity (“CPCN”) for the
transmission line, including a recommended transmission line route and viable route alternatives with the
SCC. As part of the CPCN proceeding, the SCC reviews and confirms the need for the new infrastructure
and ultimately selects and approves the final route after notice and opportunity for hearing.

The integration of intermittent renewable energy generation into the electric grid involves multiple
considerations. The generator must first be physically interconnected to the electric grid, either at the
transmission or distribution level. The developer of a generating facility typically pays the costs to
physically interconnect the resource, including any upgrades required near the point of
interconnection to ensure grid stability. As increasing volumes of renewable energy generation are
interconnected to the grid, additional system-level upgrades must be made by the Company to
integrate new resources and address grid stability and reliability issues caused by the intermittent
nature of these resources. All of these costs are incorporated in the NPV for “Total System Costs,”
as shown in Table 5-2 in the 2026 IRP.

2. Generation Re-dispatch Costs: This category represents costs resulting from real-time
variability of load and generator availability compared to day-ahead forecasted load and
generator availability.

The transmission integration costs were assessed by performing a steady state power flow and
contingency analysis when a total of 10 GW and 20 GW of solar generation is present on the
transmission grid. The analysis was performed based on real projects proposed and currently in
PJM Interconnection LLC’s (“PJM”) generation interconnection queue to best reflect the
interconnection locations, sizes, and behaviors of the solar developers. The actual proposed projects

projects, the Company has sufficient data without extrapolating for unknown projects. The resulting
power flow violations results were then used to calculate the cost per kilowatt (“kW”) of
enhancements to the Company’s transmission system.

Re-dispatch generation costs are defined as additional costs that are incurred due to the
unpredictability of events that occur during a typical day. Historically, these types of events were
driven by load variations due to actual weather that differs from the forecast. Most power system
operators assess the generation needs for a future period based on load forecasts and commit a
series of generators to be available during a given period. These committed generators are expected
to operate in an hour-to-hour sequence that minimizes total cost. Once within that period, actual load
may vary, and the committed generators may operate in a less-than-optimal hour-to-hour sequence.
The resulting additional costs due to real-time variability are known as re-dispatch costs.
Increasing amounts of intermittent generation increase the uncertainty about re-dispatch costs. The
Company performed a simulation analysis to determine the cost impact on generation operations at
varying levels of solar, onshore wind, and offshore wind penetration. To study the effects of these
intermittent resources, the Company studied historic wind speed and solar irradiance data from the
National Renewable Energy Laboratory (“NREL”).
