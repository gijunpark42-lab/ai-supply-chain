When AI finds vulnerabilities faster than organizations can fix them

October 19, 2026 | Gabriel Broner, Chief Technologist, HPE Private Cloud

Blog Post Hybrid & Private Cloud AI Security

AI is shrinking the window between vulnerability discovery and exploitation. Here is how enterprises can build security in earlier, prioritize faster, and prepare for what comes next.

In this article

AI is collapsing the gap between finding a flaw and exploiting it

Secure by design and continuous vulnerability management are now essential

Regulation, confidential computing and quantum risk demand action today

Enterprise security must move at machine speed

The gap between finding a flaw and using it is closing

In April 2026, Anthropic’s Claude Mythos found a bug in OpenBSD that had gone undetected since 1998, in one of the most heavily audited codebases in the world. In a few weeks it found thousands of previously unknown vulnerabilities across major operating systems and browsers, wrote working exploits, and chained them together with little human help. Anthropic judged the model as too dangerous to release but gave early access to a group of defenders through Project Glasswing. The lesson for enterprise IT leaders is simple. The work of finding and exploiting a vulnerability no longer requires deep expertise or a lot of time. A breach still costs the same things it always has: lost revenue, leaked intellectual property, damaged reputation, and regulatory fines. What has changed is how little time you have to prevent one. Your security process now has to move at the speed of the tools trying to break it.

Here is how mature organizations are responding.

Build security in from the first design review

Good software teams do not wait until the end to test. They design for quality, review as they go, and test continuously. Security must work the same way. When you check for weaknesses only at release, you ship the weaknesses you missed. In a world where $50 of AI spend can find a decades-old flaw, that is a poor bet.

Mature programs build the work into every stage. Architectural threat analysis finds design flaws that reduce resistance to attack. Security code reviews and static analysis catch weak code during development. Penetration testing and vulnerability assessments map real attack paths before release. A flaw fixed at design time costs far less than one fixed in production, and the end result is a stronger, more resilient product.

Manage vulnerabilities as a continuous process

Every line of code you write, and every open-source or third-party library you add, increases your exposure. That problem is growing. AI-assisted developers now write more code, and research shows that code carries roughly 10 times more security findings. A strong program handles the volume with a clear process: collect findings from multiple sources, rank them, keep them in a data lake, assign them to the right team, and track each one against a defined SLA. Without that discipline, the backlog grows faster than the fixes.

Assume your attacker has AI

Mythos shows where this is going. Anthropic locked its model down, but the capability will spread. Security researchers already describe a coming wave of AI-discovered vulnerabilities, with the time between discovery and active exploitation shrinking from months to hours. The SLAs you set last year may already be too slow.

Defenders also have better tools. The US Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA) publishes a Known Exploited Vulnerabilities (KEV) list of vulnerabilities already being exploited in real attacks. Those come first. The Exploit Prediction Scoring System (EPSS) estimates the chance a vulnerability will be exploited in the next 30 days. Used together, they prioritize your large backlog into a clear order of work: KEV-listed items first, then high-EPSS issues above 0.5. This is why our security team in HPE Private Cloud built VulnOps, a continuous vulnerability management process that lets our teams respond at the speed threats evolve.

Meet the regulation that is already in force

The EU Cyber Resilience Act (CRA) turns this into law. From September 11, 2026, manufacturers must report actively exploited vulnerabilities and severe incidents on tight timelines, with an early warning due within 24 hours. By December 11, 2027, products must meet secure-by-design requirements to carry the CE marking and be sold in Europe. The reporting duty also covers products already in the field, not only new ones. The reassuring part is that the teams doing the work above are already on the way to compliance.

Adopt confidential computing to protect data in use

For years we have protected data at rest and in motion. Confidential computing closes the remaining security gap: data in use. It isolates workloads in hardware-protected environments keeping data encrypted in system memory, where only the CPU or GPU that needs it can decrypt it. An intruding process that reaches that memory sees nothing readable. The use cases are already here. Multi-tenant environments need to shield data from other tenants and from disgruntled system administrators, and a growing number of large AI model providers now require confidential computing for on-premises deployments.

Prepare now for post-quantum

Modern security rests on public-key cryptography developed in the 1970s. You multiply two large prime numbers to create keys, and no conventional computer can factor them back apart in a useful timeframe. That has held for 50 years. Quantum computers threaten to break that, with the real potential to crack standard encryption keys in a few hours to a week. Once a stable, large-scale quantum machine exists, the foundation to today’s encryption crumbles.

This may look like a future problem, but there is a reason to start to worry. Adversaries can potentially start collecting encrypted data today, and store it to decrypt later, a practice researchers call “harvest now, decrypt later.” Anything that must stay secret for years, such as health records, financial data, and intellectual property could be at risk. That is why regulators and standards bodies are pushing products toward post-quantum cryptography, and why the work of replacing vulnerable algorithms has to start before quantum machines arrive.

The takeaway

Mythos is a preview of the environment every enterprise now operates in, where flaws are found faster than people can find them and exploited faster than teams used to patch. The old approach of scanning slowly and patching later no longer holds. Security must be designed and built early in the process. Vulnerabilities must be continuously managed. Assume your attacker has AI. Meet the regulation. Adopt confidential computing. Prepare for quantum now.

In HPE Private Cloud we are working across all these fronts, supporting our users in this journey that is getting more exciting by the minute.
