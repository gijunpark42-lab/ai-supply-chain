AI resilience: What customers should demand beyond uptime

October 12, 2026 | Jim O’Dorisio, SVP & GM, HPE Storage

Blog Post Security AI Storage Hybrid & Private Cloud Exec Authored

AI raises the resilience standard as uptime alone cannot ensure trust across connected systems and data

In this article

In the AI era, uptime is no longer the ultimate measure

Resilience disruptions are becoming harder to detect at the same time agents are moving at machine speed

Infrastructure is a key component to resilient business operations, but not the only

Customers should expect providers to provide proof and back up their claims, not rely on promises

For decades, resilience has been measured largely by two questions: Is the service available, and how quickly can it recover? Those measures still matter. But AI raises the standard.

As AI becomes embedded in business operations, resilience must account for an interconnected environment of workloads, agents, data, applications, and infrastructure. Today, an AI model can remain online, continue generating outputs, and appear healthy even after the context it depends on has been disrupted. In the AI era, resilience failures can be hard to detect and contain.

As AI becomes embedded in business operations, resilience must account for an interconnected environment of workloads, agents, data, applications, and infrastructure.

Why AI raises the resilience standard

AI systems depend on many components working together. Models, agents, data pipelines, vector databases, inference services, applications, and infrastructure can all influence the outcome. A disruption in one part of that chain may affect AI operations without producing a traditional outage.

AI can also increase the speed and reach of an operational issue. Agents can interact with data and applications and take actions at machine speed. That makes the ability to identify disruption, contain its impact, and restore the supporting environment increasingly important. The goal is not simply to keep AI running, it’s to provide a resilient foundation for trusted AI operations.

Infrastructure is the foundation, not the guarantee

Infrastructure has a critical role to play, but its role must be clear. It can help protect the workloads and data AI depends on, preserve recoverability across hybrid environments, and provide a trusted path back when disruption occurs.

What infrastructure cannot do on its own is guarantee the accuracy of data, ensure every AI decision is correct, or reverse every action an agent may have taken. Those outcomes also depend on the AI models, applications, governance, security controls, and operating processes around them.

Therefore, the value of resilient infrastructure is not that it makes AI infallible, but rather that it gives organizations a stronger foundation to protect critical dependencies, contain disruption, and recover workloads and data to a known-good state while maintaining the security, residency, and governance requirements under which the business operates.

What customers are telling us

Customers are already signaling that resilience requires more than keeping systems online. In a recent customer advisory discussion focused on cyber resilience, organizations emphasized three priorities: assurance that recovery will work, confidence in recovery outcomes, and proof that recovered data is clean and safe.

Customers also expressed interest in AI-assisted recovery decisions, but were clear that human approval must remain part of the process. The lesson is that resilience is increasingly measured by trust in the recovered environment, not just whether services remain available.

What customers should demand

Customers should expect more than promises of uptime. They should ask providers to show how the infrastructure supports trusted AI operations and be prepared to prove three things:

Protect connected workloads and data. AI does not operate in isolation. Providers should demonstrate how they protect the workloads, applications, and data dependencies that support AI as a connected environment, across hybrid infrastructure.

Recover to a known-good state within governance requirements. When disruption occurs, organizations need a trusted path back. Providers should show how workloads and data can be recovered rapidly and granularly to a known-good state, while preserving security, data residency, and governance requirements.

Demonstrate that recovery actually works. Recovery cannot be an assumption. Providers must support regular validation and realistic testing so organizations have evidence that critical services and data can be recovered as environments, dependencies, and risks change.

AI can strengthen this approach as well. Used within clear controls, it can support anomaly detection, continuous monitoring, faster remediation, and stronger evidence of recoverability. The same technology that introduces new operational complexity can also help teams respond with greater speed and precision.

In the AI era, resilience is no longer just about keeping technology available. It is about giving the business the confidence to keep moving when disruption happens.

A new measure of resilience

AI resilience is not about preventing every failure, nor is infrastructure alone responsible for every AI outcome. It is about ensuring the business has the foundation to protect what AI depends on, recover trusted workloads and data when disruption occurs, and prove that recovery will work when it matters.

The organizations that succeed with AI will look beyond uptime. They will ask whether their connected AI environment is protected, whether they can return to a known-good state within their governance requirements, and whether that recovery has been validated. Providers should be ready to prove it.

In the AI era, resilience is no longer just about keeping technology available. It is about giving the business the confidence to keep moving when disruption happens.
