When AI becomes an asset, not an expense

September 24, 2026 | Cheri Williams, SVP and GM, Private Cloud and Flex Solutions, HPE

Blog Post Exec Authored AI

As AI moves from pilots to production, enterprises must determine when consumption pricing still fits—and when sustained demand makes AI infrastructure a productive, strategic asset

In this article

As AI adoption scales, utilization becomes a key factor in the economics of ownership versus meter pricing

AI infrastructure is increasingly becoming a capital allocation decision, not just a technology decision

The value of ownership depends on keeping infrastructure productive through sustained, production-scale use

Organizations should evaluate AI by business outcomes, security, governance, and long-term cost—not token prices alone

September 24, 2026 – Conversations about AI costs often begin with token prices and access to the latest cloud models. Those factors matter, but they can obscure the larger question: as AI moves into production, how can enterprises meet growing demand economically, predictably, and at scale?

The issue becomes more urgent as demand grows steady and business-critical . A consumption-only approach can turn AI into a volatile monthly expense that is difficult to forecast as usage, workloads, and model requirements evolve.

AI is moving from isolated pilots to production portfolios that include assistants, retrieval and knowledge systems, and agentic applications. Across customer service, IT, research, and business processes, these applications execute multi-step workflows and create recurring demand for models, data, and tools.

This is already starting to happen. Deloitte’s 2026 State of AI in the Enterprise report reflects what many leaders are seeing: worker access to AI rose 5% in 2025, and the share of companies with at least 40% of their AI projects in production is expected to double within six months.

When AI becomes a portfolio of always-on workloads rather than a collection of experiments, the economics change. Consumption pricing preserves flexibility and limits commitment. But once usage is steady, predictable, and sufficient to keep capacity productive, leaders should ask a different question: Does it still make sense to buy AI one request at a time, or is it time to invest in capacity the enterprise can optimize and control?

This is not a binary cloud-versus- on-premises debate; it is a workload-by-workload capital allocation decision. Leaders should estimate demand over the next 12 to 18 months, assess how consistently capacity will be used, and determine whether shared infrastructure can spread fixed costs across a broader portfolio of productive workloads.

Owning capacity can also strengthen control

Additionally, owning AI capacity can give enterprises greater control over where sensitive data is processed, who can access models and infrastructure, how workloads are isolated, and how activity is logged and audited. It can support data sovereignty and regulatory requirements as well by keeping governed workloads within defined environments. But ownership also transfers responsibility: the enterprise must secure, patch, monitor, and govern the platform throughout its lifecycle. Those capabilities and costs belong in the ownership decision from the start.

The question is how much you run

Ownership is not automatically the lower-cost answer. It only makes sense when an enterprise can keep capacity productive.

Every organization has a crossover point: the level of sustained utilization at which owning capacity can become more economical than buying it one request at a time. That threshold is unique to each enterprise and depends on model mix, input and output token volumes, performance requirements, system design, energy costs, and the supporting operating model.

A retrieval-heavy knowledge system can have a very different cost profile from a simple assistant because it may process far more context for every interaction. Agentic workflows can be different again: a single business task may involve repeated reasoning, retrieval, model calls, and tool use. That is why generic cost benchmarks are not enough. Enterprises need to model their actual workloads, understand expected demand, and size capacity accordingly.

At the right utilization level, ownership can deliver lower effective cost, more predictable spending, and tighter control over data, access, compliance, and workload placement. The value is not only economic; it is the ability to operate AI capacity as a governed strategic asset.

Ownership only works when it is put to work

The capital decision is only half the equation. Even when ownership is economically justified, value depends on moving workloads into production quickly and keeping the capacity productively employed.

Making that happen requires an operating model that connects infrastructure to adoption, security, and business outcomes. It should define accountability, govern data and model use, monitor access and utilization, and continually prioritize high-value use cases.

The goal is to create value early and compound it over time. Leaders should measure utilization, identify idle capacity, and continually add high-value workloads to the platform. Without that discipline, the organization may never realize the economics that justified the investment. With it, AI capacity becomes a productive asset the business can optimize, expand, and use to deliver measurable outcomes.

Three questions to ask

Before committing capital, leaders should ask three questions:

1. Is demand becoming steady, predictable, and large enough to justify dedicated capacity?

2. At what level of usage does ownership make economic sense?

3. Can we keep capacity productive while enforcing security, governance, compliance, and responsible-use controls?

Make the shift deliberately

Organizations that create the most value will look beyond token prices and model access. They will align ownership with sustained demand, keep capacity productive, and use direct control of infrastructure to enforce security, governance, compliance, and responsible AI across the lifecycle.

That is when AI stops being a bill and becomes an asset.
